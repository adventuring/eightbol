Welcome, storyteller! This manual is your personal guide to mastering the Fountain scripting system
 for \Phantasia. Whether you are a seasoned game writer or just beginning your journey, you will
  find here the tools and inspiration to craft dramatic, engaging cut scenes and interactive
   moments that will captivate players.

You are the director, the playwright, and the architect of emotion. As you read, imagine orchestrating the kind of memorable scenes found in the greatest action role-playing games. This manual is written for the creative mind intent on bringing their vision to life, regardless of gender or background.

Each chapter introduces a set of features, explains their purpose, and provides clear, practical examples. You will find pop culture vignettes, technical notes, and references to classic games to help you see how Fountain scripting can elevate your storytelling.

\section{The Evolution of Skyline-Tool's Fountain Scripting}

The Fountain scripting system used by Skyline-Tool in \Phantasia\ represents the culmination of a journey that began with a project called "Skyline." Originally conceived as an Atari 2600 game, Skyline was intended to push the boundaries of storytelling on the classic console. However, during development, my house was destroyed by fire, resulting in the loss of both the work-in-progress and my immediate interest in continuing the project.

Years later, while developing \textit{Grizzards} (2022, AtariAge), I found myself needing to create a simple scripting system for non-player characters. This basic system, though limited, proved essential for bringing the game's world to life. When I began work on \Phantasia, I knew I wanted to dramatically expand the expressive capabilities available for storytelling.

It was during this process that I realized the potential of embedding game scripting commands within standard Fountain screenplay format. This approach allows writers to use familiar, established screenplay conventions while providing powerful game control capabilities through Skyline-Tool's extensions. The result is a scripting facility that represents a massively overpowered superset of the scripting capabilities found in the \textit{Grizzards} engine.

Under the hood, Stagehand's Forth interpreter serves as the execution engine, interpreting these Fountain screenplay scripts and translating the embedded game commands into actions. This combination of standard Fountain screenplay syntax with Skyline-Tool's game-specific extensions creates a unique and accessible system for interactive storytelling.

\section{The Simplest Possible Scene}

Here's a complete, working Fountain scene:

\begin{FountainExample}
\slugline{INT TAVERN - MAIN ROOM}

\stagedir{Open on: INNKEEPER is at "Bar Counter". INNKEEPER starts dancing. Then...}
\stagedir{Enter PLAYER at "Tavern Door". PLAYER faces west. Beat. PLAYER faces east.}

\stagedir{PLAYER walks to "Bar Counter".}

\speaker{INNKEEPER}
\dialogue{Welcome, traveler! What can I get you?}

\speaker{PLAYER}
\dialogue{I'll have an ale, please.}

\sceneendcmd{> FADE TO BLACK}
\end{FountainExample}

That's it! This scene:
\begin{itemize}
\item Sets up a location (tavern)
\item Brings in the player character
\item Has dialogue between two characters
\item Ends with a fade transition
\end{itemize}

\section{What is Fountain Scripting?}

\gls{fountain} is the established markup language used for writing Hollywood screenplays. Skyline-Tool extends this format to support interactive game content, allowing developers to write \gls{fountain} screenplays that can control \gls{dialogue}, \gls{character} movement, \gls{camera} control, and game logic in retro games like \Phantasia.

The language is designed to be accessible to writers and designers who may not have programming experience, while still providing the power and flexibility needed for complex interactive storytelling.

\section{Fountain for Screenwriters}
\label{sec:fountain-for-screenwriters}

If you're familiar with standard Fountain screenplay format, you'll find that \Phantasia's Fountain implementation extends the traditional format with game-specific features. This section explains the key differences and similarities.

\subsection{What's the Same}

\textbf{Standard Fountain Elements That Work:}
\begin{description}
\item[Scene Headers] \fountain{INT/EXT LOCATION - TIME} format
\item[Character Names] Uppercase character names for dialogue
\item[Dialogue] Standard dialogue formatting with character names
\item[Parentheticals] Character actions and emotions in parentheses (\gls{parenthetical})
\item[Transitions] Standard transitions like \fountain{FADE OUT}, \fountain{CUT TO}
\item[Notes] Comments in double brackets \fountain{[[ Note ]]}
\end{description}

\begin{SimpleExample}
\begin{FountainExample}
\slugline{INT ADDAMS MANSION - PARLOR}

\speaker{WEDNESDAY}
\dialogue{The baby is plotting something. I can see it in his eyes.}

\speaker{PUGSLEY}
\dialogue{Maybe he's planning to take over the world. That would be cool.}

\stagedir{PUGSLEY walks to "Armchair". PUGSLEY faces north.}

\stagedir{[[ This is a standard Fountain note ]]}

\sceneendcmd{FADE OUT}
\end{FountainExample}
\end{SimpleExample}

\subsection{What's Different}

\textbf{Game-Specific Extensions:}
\begin{description}
\item[Map Integration] Scene headers load actual game maps
\item[Character Positioning] Explicit character entry and movement commands
\item[Camera Control] Detailed camera movement and framing
\item[Game Variables] Interactive state management (\gls{interpolation})
\item[Conditional Logic] Branching dialogue and scenes (\gls{branching})
\item[Mathematical Expressions] Complex calculations and formulas (\gls{mathematical-expression})
\end{description}

\begin{GameScenario}
\begin{FountainExample}
\slugline{INT TAVERN - MAIN ROOM}

\stagedir{Open on: INNKEEPER is at "Bar Counter". Then...}
\stagedir{Enter PLAYER at "Tavern Door".}

\stagedir{Set \$gold to 100.}

\speaker{INNKEEPER}
\dialogue{Welcome! A room costs 50 gold pieces.}

\speaker{PLAYER}
\dialogue{I have 75 gold pieces.}
\stagedir{When \$gold is greater than or equal to 50, Set \$gold to the difference of \$gold less 50. When \$gold is greater than or equal to 50, INNKEEPER: Here's your key.}
\stagedir{Unless \$gold is greater than or equal to 50, INNKEEPER: Sorry, you can't afford it.}
\end{FountainExample}
\end{GameScenario}

\subsection{Key Differences Summary}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{|l|l|l|}
\hline
\textbf{Feature} & \textbf{Standard Fountain} & \textbf{Phantasia Fountain} \\
\hline
Scene Headers & Narrative only & Loads game maps (\gls{slugline}) \\
Character Entry & Implied & Explicit \fountain{Enter} commands (\gls{character-entry}) \\
Movement & Described in action & Programmatic \fountain{walks} commands (\gls{character-movement}) \\
Camera & Not specified & Detailed \fountain{Cut}, \fountain{Truck}, \fountain{Dolly} (\gls{camera-movement}) \\
Variables & Not available & Full \gls{variable} system \\
Conditionals & Not available & \fountain{When}, \fountain{Unless}, \fountain{If} logic (\gls{conditional-logic}) \\
Math & Not available & Complex \gls{mathematical-expression}s \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsection{Migration Tips for Screenwriters}

\textbf{Converting Standard Fountain Scripts:}
\begin{description}
\item[Add Character Entries] Insert \fountain{Enter CHARACTER at "Location"} for each character (\gls{metadata})
\item[Specify Camera Work] Add \fountain{Cut to center on CHARACTER} for focus (\gls{compilation})\footnote{For comprehensive camera control commands, see \autoref{ch:camera}.}
\item[Define Game State] Add variables for tracking story progress (\gls{workflow})
\item[Add Interactivity] Use conditionals for branching dialogue (\gls{debugging})
\item[Test Map Integration] Ensure scene headers match available game maps (\gls{validation})
\end{description}

\subsubsection{Screenwriter Best Practices}
\begin{itemize}
\item Start with your existing Fountain screenplay structure (\gls{walk})
\item Add game-specific elements gradually (\gls{frame})
\item Use variables to track character relationships and story progress (\gls{fade})
\item Test interactive elements thoroughly (\gls{exit})
\item Keep the narrative flow while adding game mechanics (\gls{facing})
\end{itemize}


\begin{AntiPattern}
\subsubsection{Common Screenwriter Mistakes}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Forgetting character entries}: Characters can't speak without being entered (\gls{modifier})
\item \textbf{Ignoring camera work}: Scenes need explicit camera direction (\gls{metadata})
\item \textbf{Over-complicating interactivity}: Start simple, add complexity later (\gls{compilation})
\item \textbf{Using non-existent maps}: Scene headers must match actual game files (\gls{workflow})
\item \textbf{Forgetting game constraints}: Consider technical limitations (\gls{navigation})
\end{itemize}
\end{AntiPattern}

\subsection{Example: Converting a Standard Scene}

\subsubsection{Original Standard Fountain}
\begin{verbatim}
EXT GARDEN - DAY

A desperate battle rages. Ducks swarm from all directions, intent on stealing vegetables. A diseased orange dog named Methound fights alongside the player.

PLAYER
There's too many of them!

METHOUND
*shakes violently* I need... I need my medicine!

DUCK1
*quacks menacingly*

PLAYER
Hold the line, Methound! Protect the veg!
\end{verbatim}

\subsubsection{Converted for Phantasia}
\begin{FountainExample}
\slugline{EXT GARDEN - VEGETABLE PATCH}
\stagedir{Open on: PLAYER is at "Garden Center". PLAYER equips CORKGUN. Then...}
\stagedir{Enter METHOUND at "Dog House". METHOUND looks sweating. Enter MALLARD at "Bread Hiding Spot". MALLARD faces west. Enter DRAKE at "Sky Position". DRAKE faces south. Enter GOOSE at "Ground Position". GOOSE faces east.}

\speaker{PLAYER}
\dialogue{There's too many of them!}

\stagedir{METHOUND looks sweating.}

\speaker{METHOUND}
\dialogue{I need... I need my medicine!}

\stagedir{BREADDUCK looks sweating.}

\speaker{PLAYER}
\dialogue{Hold the line, Methound! Protect the veg!}

\stagedir{Set \$battle-intensity to 3. PLAYER looks sweating.}

\sceneendcmd{> FADE OUT}
\end{FountainExample}
This conversion adds the necessary game-specific elements while preserving the original narrative structure and dialogue.
\section{Progressive Enhancement Philosophy}
Fountain scripts follow a progressive enhancement approach:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Level 1 - Basic}: Simple dialogue and character movement (Chapter \ref{ch:quickstart})
\item \textbf{Level 2 - Intermediate}: Scene structure and camera control (Chapters \ref{ch:scene-management}, \ref{ch:camera})
\item \textbf{Level 3 - Advanced}: Variables and conditional logic (Chapters \ref{ch:variables}, \ref{ch:conditionals})
\item \textbf{Level 4 - Expert}: Complex game state and mathematical expressions (Chapters \ref{ch:math}, \ref{ch:advanced})
\end{itemize}

Each level builds upon the previous one, allowing you to start simple and add complexity as needed.

\section{Design Principles}
The Fountain scripting system follows several key principles:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Readability First}: Scripts should read like natural prose
\item \textbf{Progressive Complexity}: Start simple, add features as needed
\item \textbf{Consistent Syntax}: Similar operations use similar patterns
\item \textbf{Error Prevention}: Clear syntax reduces common mistakes
\item \textbf{Performance Aware}: Efficient execution on limited hardware
\end{itemize}

Every Fountain script consists of these core elements:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Scene Headers}: Define locations and settings
\item \textbf{Character Entries}: Introduce actors to the scene
\item \textbf{Dialogue}: Spoken text between characters
\item \textbf{Stage Directions}: Camera, movement, and game logic
\end{itemize}

\label{ch:scene-management}
\sectionbreak

\section{Prerequisites}

Scenes\index{scene}\index{scene!fundamentals} are the fundamental building blocks of Fountain scripts. Each scene represents a distinct location or moment in your story, with its own map, characters, and atmosphere.

\section{Scene Headers}
\paragraphbreak

Scene headers\index{scene!headers}\index{slugline} are the foundation of every Fountain script. They define not only the narrative location but also the actual game map that will be loaded.

\subsection{Basic Scene Syntax}
\paragraphbreak

\begin{SimpleExample}
\begin{FountainExample}
\slugline{INT ATSIRAV - ATSIRAV TOWN HALL}
\end{FountainExample}
\end{SimpleExample}

\begin{Important}
There must be \textbf{zero or one} \fountain{Open on:} section per scene. All initial placements and states must be included in a single \fountain{Open on:} block. Multiple \fountain{Open on:} blocks in the same scene are not allowed and will cause errors.
\end{Important}

This simple line does several things:
\begin{itemize}
\item Loads the map file \texttt{Source/Maps/Atsirav/AtsiravTownHall.tmx}
\item Sets the scene context for all subsequent actions
\item Establishes the coordinate system for character positioning
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Fullscreen BLOB Scene Syntax}
\paragraphbreak

For non-interactive fullscreen interstitials, use a slugline ending in \fountain{(BLOB)}:

\begin{SimpleExample}
\begin{FountainExample}
\slugline{INT TITLE CARD (BLOB)}
\end{FountainExample}
\end{SimpleExample}

This compiles to a blob lookup symbol named \texttt{Blob\_TitleCard\_ID} and displays that BLOB screen until dismissed by dialogue-style input (or auto-advance timeout when enabled).

\noindent See \autoref{app:lexer-syntax} for every lexer-recognized line form and additional worked BLOB examples.

\subsubsection{Scene Header Best Practices}
\begin{itemize}
\item Use clear, descriptive location names
\item Follow the exact format: \fountain{LOCATION - MAP NAME}
\item Ensure the map file exists in the correct directory
\item Use consistent naming conventions across your project
\end{itemize}


\begin{AntiPattern}
\subsubsection{Common Scene Header Mistakes}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Missing separator}: \fountain{INT ATSIRAV ATSIRAV TOWN HALL} (no hyphen)
\item \textbf{Incorrect format}: \fountain{INT ATSIRAV - ATSIRAV TOWN HALL - DECK} (too many parts)
\item \textbf{Non-existent map}: \fountain{INT FANTASY - CASTLE} (map file doesn't exist)
\item \textbf{Inconsistent spacing}: \fountain{INT ATSIRAV-ATSIRAV TOWN HALL} (no spaces around hyphen)
\end{itemize}
\end{AntiPattern}

\subsection{Map Label Conversion: PascalCase to Display Format}

The map name portion of the slugline (after the hyphen) corresponds to the actual map file name, but with a specific conversion applied for display purposes.

\begin{description}
\item[File Name Format] Map files use PascalCase naming: \texttt{ThatRoom.tmx}, \texttt{MainHall.tmx}, \texttt{SecretPassage.tmx}
\item[Display Format] The parser converts PascalCase to ``CAPS AND SPACES'' for the slugline display
\item[Digit Handling] Any digits at the end of a map name are preserved but will not appear on the player's screen
\end{description}

\begin{SimpleExample}
\begin{description}
\item[\texttt{ThatRoom.tmx}] → \fountain{INT LOCATION - THAT ROOM}
\item[\texttt{MainHall.tmx}] → \fountain{INT LOCATION - MAIN HALL}
\item[\texttt{SecretPassage.tmx}] → \fountain{INT LOCATION - SECRET PASSAGE}
\item[\texttt{ThatRoom1.tmx}] → \fountain{INT LOCATION - THAT ROOM1} (digit preserved but hidden from player)
\item[\texttt{MainHall2.tmx}] → \fountain{INT LOCATION - MAIN HALL2} (digit preserved but hidden from player)
\end{description}
\end{SimpleExample}

\subsubsection{Map Naming Best Practices}
\begin{itemize}
\item Use descriptive PascalCase names for map files
\item Avoid special characters in file names (except digits at the end)
\item Use digits at the end only when you need multiple variants of the same location
\item Remember that digits will be preserved in the slugline but hidden from players
\end{itemize}


\begin{AntiPattern}
\subsubsection{Map Naming Mistakes}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Using spaces in file names}: \texttt{That Room.tmx} (will cause errors)
\item \textbf{Using lowercase}: \texttt{thatroom.tmx} (conversion will be inconsistent)
\item \textbf{Special characters}: \texttt{That-Room.tmx} (may cause parsing issues)
\item \textbf{Digits in middle}: \texttt{That1Room.tmx} (will display as ``THAT1 ROOM'')
\end{itemize}
\end{AntiPattern}

\subsection{Scene State Management: Unlike Film Scripts}

In traditional film scripts, you can assume that actors and props remain in place between scenes. In Fountain scripting for games, \textbf{every scene must explicitly set up its own actors and their positions}.

\subsubsection{Scene State Management Best Practices}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Always enter characters explicitly}: Use \fountain{Enter CHARACTER at "Position"} for every character\footnote{For detailed character entry syntax and best practices, see \autoref{sec:character-entry}.}
\item \textbf{Set initial positions}: Place characters where they need to be for the scene
\item \textbf{Track character states}: Remember where characters ended in previous scenes
\item \textbf{Reset when returning}: Explicitly place characters back when returning to a scene
\end{itemize}


\begin{AntiPattern}
\subsubsection{Film Script Assumptions to Avoid}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Assuming characters stay put}: Characters don't automatically remain in previous positions
\item \textbf{Omitting character entry}: Every scene needs explicit character setup
\item \textbf{Forgetting state tracking}: You must remember where characters ended
\item \textbf{Implicit positioning}: All positions must be explicitly stated
\end{itemize}
\end{AntiPattern}

\subsection{Example: Film Script vs. Fountain Script}

\subsubsection{Traditional Film Script (Incorrect for Games)}
\begin{verbatim}
INT FASHION OFFICE - MAIN ROOM

Patty sits at the desk. Edwina enters and joins her.

PATTY
How was your day, darling?

EDWINA
(sitting down)
Absolutely fabulous, sweetie. Mind if I join you?

PATTY
Not at all, darling.

INT FASHION OFFICE - MAIN ROOM (LATER)

Patty and Edwina are still at the desk, now with champagne.

PATTY
So what brings you to the office?
\end{verbatim}

\subsubsection{Fountain Script (Correct for Games)}
\begin{FountainExample}
\slugline{INT FASHION OFFICE - MAIN ROOM}

\stagedir{Open on: PATSY is at "Design Desk". Then...}
\stagedir{Enter EDWINA at "Office Entrance". EDWINA walks to "Guest Chair".}

\speaker{PATSY}
\dialogue{How was your day, darling?}

\speaker{EDWINA}
\dialogue{Absolutely fabulous, sweetie. Mind if I join you?}

\stagedir{EDWINA walks to "Guest Chair". EDWINA faces north.}

\speaker{PATSY}
\dialogue{Not at all, darling.}

\stagedir{EDWINA equips champagne. PATSY equips champagne.}

\speaker{PATSY}
\dialogue{So what brings you to the office?}
\end{FountainExample}

\subsubsection{Key Differences}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Film Script}: Assumes Patty stays at the desk between scenes
\item \textbf{Fountain Script}: Explicitly places Patty at the desk in each scene
\item \textbf{Film Script}: Implies Edwina is still there from previous scene
\item \textbf{Fountain Script}: Explicitly enters Edwina and positions her
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Dramatic Pacing and Character Entrances}

In dramatic storytelling, character entrances should feel natural and purposeful. Fountain scripts should follow realistic pacing rather than having characters immediately appear and speak.

\subsubsection{Dramatic Pacing Best Practices}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Use ``Open on'' for established scenes}: Start with characters already in position when the scene begins
\item \textbf{Allow entrance time}: Characters should enter, then have a beat or action before speaking
\item \textbf{Speak from doorways}: Characters can speak immediately upon arrival if dramatically appropriate
\item \textbf{Use "Then..." sequences}: Build dramatic tension with proper timing
\item \textbf{Add beats and pauses}: Give characters time to react and process
\end{itemize}


\begin{AntiPattern}
\subsubsection{Poor Dramatic Pacing to Avoid}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Immediate entrances and dialogue}: Characters shouldn't enter and speak instantly
\item \textbf{No dramatic setup}: Scenes should establish atmosphere before action
\item \textbf{Missing beats}: Characters need time to react and process events
\item \textbf{Unrealistic timing}: Actions should follow natural dramatic flow
\end{itemize}
\end{AntiPattern}

\subsection{Example: Proper Dramatic Pacing}

\subsubsection{Poor Pacing (Avoid)}
\begin{FountainExample}
\slugline{INT MILK BAR - MAIN AREA}

\stagedir{Open on: ANJU is at "Table 1". ANJU looks surprised. Then...}
\stagedir{Enter KAFEI at "Shop Entrance". KAFEI walks to "Table 1".}

\speaker{ANJU}
\dialogue{Hey, how's it going?}
\end{FountainExample}

\subsubsection{Better Pacing (Recommended)}
\begin{FountainExample}
\slugline{INT MILK BAR - MAIN AREA}

\stagedir{Open on: ANJU is at "Table 1". ANJU looks surprised. Then...}
\stagedir{Enter KAFEI at "Shop Entrance". KAFEI looks surprised.}
\stagedir{KAFEI walks to "Table 1".}

\speaker{KAFEI}

\subsection{Character Placement Requirement}

\textbf{Critical Rule}: Every character who speaks or acts in a scene must be explicitly placed in the scene first. Characters cannot simply appear and speak without being established.

\begin{AntiPattern}
\subsubsection{Common Pitfall - Characters Speaking Without Placement}
\begin{FountainExample}
\slugline{INT TAVERN - MAIN ROOM}

\speaker{INNKEEPER}
\dialogue{Welcome, traveler! What can I get you?}
\end{FountainExample}

\textbf{Problem}: The INNKEEPER speaks without being placed in the scene. Where did they come from?
\end{AntiPattern}

\subsubsection{Proper Character Placement}
\begin{description}
\item[Use "Open on" for established characters] \fountain{Open on: INNKEEPER is at "Bar Counter". Then...}
\item[Use "Enter" for new arrivals] \fountain{Enter PLAYER at "Tavern Door".}
\item[Place all characters before dialogue] Every speaking character must be positioned first
\item[Consider dramatic timing] Characters should enter, then have a beat before speaking
\end{description}


\subsubsection{Corrected Example}
\begin{FountainExample}
\slugline{INT TAVERN - MAIN ROOM}

\stagedir{Open on: INNKEEPER is at "Bar Counter". INNKEEPER starts dancing. Then...}
\stagedir{Enter PLAYER at "Tavern Door". PLAYER faces west. Beat. PLAYER faces east. Beat. PLAYER walks to "Bar Counter".}

\stagedir{INNKEEPER stops dancing. INNKEEPER looks surprised.}

\speaker{INNKEEPER}
\dialogue{Welcome, traveler! What can I get you?}
\end{FountainExample}


\subsection{The Four Pillars of Proper Character Introduction}

Every scene must follow these four essential rules for believable, dramatic storytelling:

\subsubsection{1. Correct Character Introduction}
\begin{description}
\item[Established characters] Use \fountain{Open on: CHARACTER is at "Location"} for characters already in the scene
\item[New arrivals] Use \fountain{Enter CHARACTER at "Door/Entrance"} for characters entering the scene
\item[Never enter at furniture] Characters cannot enter at chairs, tables, counters, or stools
\item[Logical entry points] Use doors, entrances, or other logical entry locations
\end{description}


\subsubsection{2. Meaningful Positioning and Movement}
\begin{description}
\item[Separate characters] Never place multiple characters in the same tile/location
\item[Use walking commands] \fountain{CHARACTER walks to "Location"} to move characters to their destinations
\item[Consider spatial relationships] Position characters where they would logically be
\item[Plan character flow] Design how characters will move through the scene
\end{description}


\subsubsection{3. Proper Facing Directions}
\begin{description}
\item[Set facing directions] Use \fountain{CHARACTER faces north/south/east/west} to show attention
\item[Face toward action] Characters should face toward the person they're talking to or the action they're watching
\item[Use cardinal directions only] Characters can only face north, south, east, or west
\item[Show character focus] Facing reveals what characters are paying attention to
\end{description}


\subsubsection{4. Dramatic Beats and Timing}
\begin{description}
\item[Add beats after entrances] Give characters time to react after entering
\item[Use "Then..." sequences] Build dramatic tension with proper timing
\item[Include character reactions] \fountain{CHARACTER looks surprised} or other emotional states
\item[Allow processing time] Characters need time to react to events and dialogue
\item[Wait after emotions] Add a beat after characters show emotions before they move or speak
\end{description}

\item[No facing directions] Characters don't face each other
\item[No dramatic beats] Characters speak immediately without setup

\subsection{Why These Rules Matter}

\subsubsection{Technical Consequences}
\begin{description}
\item[Game engine errors] Characters not properly placed may not appear or may appear in wrong locations
\item[Collision detection] Multiple characters in same tile can cause visual glitches or blocking
\item[Animation issues] Characters without facing directions may not animate properly
\item[State confusion] Game engine cannot track character positions without explicit placement
\end{description}

\subsubsection{Dramatic Consequences}
\begin{description}
\item[Unbelievable scenes] Characters appearing out of nowhere break immersion
\item[Poor pacing] No dramatic beats make scenes feel rushed and artificial
\item[Spatial confusion] Characters not facing each other makes dialogue feel disconnected
\item[Missing tension] No reaction time removes emotional impact from events
\end{description}

\subsubsection{Professional Standards}
\begin{description}
\item[Player expectations] Players expect characters to behave logically and dramatically
\item[Maintenance] Well-structured scenes are easier to debug and modify
\end{description}

\subsection{Tile Occupancy Rules: One Character Per Tile}

\textbf{Critical Rule}: Only one character can occupy a tile at any time. If a character enters at a location, they must move away before another character can enter at or walk to that same location.

\begin{AntiPattern}
\textbf{Incorrect - Multiple Characters at Same Tile:}
\begin{FountainExample}
\slugline{INT TAVERN - MAIN ROOM}

\stagedir{Enter PLAYER at "Tavern Door".}
\stagedir{Enter ALICE at "Tavern Door".}

\speaker{ALICE}
\dialogue{Hello there!}
\end{FountainExample}

\textbf{Problem}: Both PLAYER and ALICE are trying to occupy the "Tavern Door" tile simultaneously.
\end{AntiPattern}

\textbf{✓ Best Practice}
\textbf{Correct - Sequential Entry and Movement:}
\begin{FountainExample}
\slugline{INT TAVERN - MAIN ROOM}

\stagedir{Enter PLAYER at "Tavern Door". PLAYER faces west. Beat. PLAYER faces east. Beat. PLAYER walks to "Bar Stool 1".}
\stagedir{Enter ALICE at "Tavern Door". ALICE looks surprised. ALICE walks to "Bar Stool 2".}

\speaker{ALICE}
\dialogue{Hello there!}
\end{FountainExample}


\subsection{Common Tile Occupancy Scenarios}

\textbf{✓ Best Practice}
\textbf{Scenario 1: Multiple Characters Entering Through Same Door}
\begin{description}
\item[Wrong] Both characters enter at the same door simultaneously
\item[Right] First character enters, moves away, then second character enters
\item[Example] PLAYER enters at "Door", walks to "Position 1", then ALICE enters at "Door"
\end{description}


\textbf{✓ Best Practice}
\textbf{Scenario 2: Character Walking to Occupied Tile}
\begin{description}
\item[Wrong] Character walks to a tile already occupied by another character
\item[Right] Character walks to a different, unoccupied tile
\item[Example] PLAYER is at "Position 1", ALICE walks to "Position 2" (not "Position 1")
\end{description}


\textbf{✓ Best Practice}
\textbf{Scenario 3: Character Movement After Entry}
\begin{description}
\item[Required] Characters must move away from entry points after entering
\item[Reason] Entry points (doors, entrances) need to remain clear for other characters
\item[Example] PLAYER enters at "Door", immediately walks to "Interior Position"
\end{description}

\subsection{Voice-Over and Off-Camera Characters}

\textbf{Important Exception}: Voice-over (VO) and off-camera (OC) characters do not need to be physically placed in the scene. They have no physical presence and can speak without being positioned.

\textbf{✓ Best Practice}
\textbf{Voice-Over Characters:}
\begin{description}
\item[No physical placement] VO characters don't need \fountain{Enter} or \fountain{Open on} commands
\item[Use (VO) designation] Mark character names with \fountain{(VO)} for voice-over (\gls{voice-over})
\item[Examples] \fountain{NARRATOR (VO)}, \fountain{JARVIS (VO)}, \fountain{SHIP'S AI (VO)}
\item[Can speak anytime] VO characters can speak without being in the scene
\end{description}


\textbf{✓ Best Practice}
\textbf{Off-Camera Characters:}
\begin{description}
\item[No physical placement] OC characters don't need \fountain{Enter} or \fountain{Open on} commands
\item[Use (O/C) designation] Mark character names with \fountain{(O/C)} for off-camera (\gls{off-camera})
\item[Examples] \fountain{BOB (O/C)}, \fountain{ANNOUNCER (O/C)}, \fountain{VOICE (O/C)}
\item[Can speak anytime] OC characters can speak without being in the scene
\end{description}


\begin{AntiPattern}
\textbf{Incorrect - Placing VO/OC Characters:}
\begin{FountainExample}
\slugline{INT TAVERN - MAIN ROOM}

\stagedir{Enter PLAYER at "Tavern Door".}
\stagedir{Enter NARRATOR at "Nowhere".}

\speaker{NARRATOR}
\dialogue{The hero enters the tavern, seeking adventure.}
\end{FountainExample}

\textbf{Problem}: NARRATOR is a voice-over character and doesn't need physical placement.
\end{AntiPattern}

\textbf{✓ Best Practice}
\textbf{Correct - VO/OC Character Usage:}
\begin{FountainExample}
\slugline{INT TAVERN - MAIN ROOM}

\stagedir{Enter PLAYER at "Tavern Door". PLAYER faces west. Beat. PLAYER faces east.}

\speaker{NARRATOR (VO)}
\dialogue{The hero enters the tavern, seeking adventure.}

\speaker{PLAYER}
\dialogue{Hello? Anyone here?}

\speaker{INNKEEPER (O/C)}
\dialogue{Coming! Just a moment!}
\end{FountainExample}

\subsection{Returning to Scenes: State Management}

When returning to a scene, you must explicitly place characters where they ended in the previous scene:

\begin{SimpleExample}
\begin{FountainExample}
\slugline{INT TAVERN - MAIN ROOM}

\stagedir{Open on: ALICE is at "Bar Stool 1". BOB is at "Bar Stool 2". Then...}
\stagedir{ALICE equips DRINK. BOB equips DRINK.}

\speaker{ALICE}
\dialogue{So what brings you to town?}
\end{FountainExample}
\end{SimpleExample}
\textbf{Important:} The game engine doesn't remember character positions between scenes. You must explicitly set up the scene state each time.
\textbf{✓ Best Practice}
\textbf{State Management Tips:}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Track character positions}: Note where each character ended
\item \textbf{Remember equipment}: Characters don't keep items between scenes
\item \textbf{Reset emotions}: Character expressions don't persist
\item \textbf{Plan re-entry}: Design scenes to be self-contained
\end{itemize}

\section{Prerequisites}

The camera\index{camera}\index{camera!control} system in \Phantasia\ provides cinematic control over what the player sees, allowing for dramatic storytelling and clear focus on important elements.

\section{Basic Camera Movement}
\paragraphbreak

For quick cuts and immediate repositioning:

\begin{SimpleExample}
\begin{FountainExample}
\stagedir{Cut to include ACTOR.}
\stagedir{Cut to center on "Location".}
\stagedir{Frame ACTOR and ACTOR.}
\end{FountainExample}
\end{SimpleExample}

These commands provide immediate camera repositioning without smooth movement.

\textbf{✓ Best Practice}
\textbf{Camera Movement Best Practices:}
\begin{description}
\item[Use \fountain{Cut to}] for instant camera changes
\item[Use \fountain{Frame}] to include multiple subjects
\item[Use \fountain{Close on}] for dramatic focus on single subjects
\item[Plan camera movements] to enhance storytelling, not distract from it
\end{description}


\begin{AntiPattern}
\textbf{Camera Movement Mistakes:}
\begin{description}
\item[Excessive movement] Too many camera changes can be disorienting
\item[Inconsistent framing] Switching between close-ups and wide shots without purpose
\item[Ignoring dialogue] Camera should support, not compete with, character speech
\item[Forgetting player perspective] Camera should help players understand the scene
\end{description}
\end{AntiPattern}

\subsection{Camera Movement in 2D Worlds}

The virtual camera moves around the fixed-size, tile-based, two-dimensional world. While the viewport size is fixed by the screen, the camera can move to show different areas:

\begin{SimpleExample}
\begin{FountainExample}
\stagedir{Truck left.}
\stagedir{Truck left to include ACTOR.}
\stagedir{Truck left to center on "Location Name".}
\stagedir{Truck 14 left.}
\stagedir{Dolly up to center on ACTOR.}
\stagedir{Dolly down to include "Location Name".}
\end{FountainExample}
\end{SimpleExample}

The engine automatically corrects direction if you specify the ``wrong'' way to reach a target. It also combines truck and dolly movements as needed.

\textbf{✓ Best Practice}
\textbf{Smooth Camera Best Practices:}
\begin{itemize}
\item Use \fountain{Truck} for horizontal movement (left/right)
\item Use \fountain{Dolly} for vertical movement (up/down)
\item Specify distance (e.g., \fountain{Truck 14 left}) for precise control
\item Use \fountain{to include} to show multiple subjects, \fountain{to center on} to focus on one
\item Remember the viewport size is fixed - no zooming in or out
\end{itemize}


\begin{AntiPattern}
\textbf{Smooth Camera Mistakes:}
\begin{description}
\item[Over-complicated movements] Don't chain too many truck/dolly commands
\item[Inconsistent timing] Smooth movements should have purpose and rhythm
\item[Ignoring player experience] Too many camera movements can be annoying and disorienting
\item[Forgetting context] Camera should match the emotional tone of the scene
\item[Expecting zoom] The viewport size is fixed - no close-ups or wide shots
\end{description}
\end{AntiPattern}

\section{Camera Behavior Rules}

\begin{itemize}
\item The camera automatically tries to include speaking characters
\item Off-camera speakers (marked with \fountain{(O/C)}) don't trigger camera movement
\item The camera respects map boundaries and won't move outside the playable area
\item Multiple camera commands in sequence are executed in order
\end{itemize}

\section{Character Naming System}
\paragraphbreak

The character naming system uses two distinct names for each character:

\begin{description}
\item[Display Name] The full, proper name that appears on screen (e.g., ``King T'Challa'', ``Captain Picard'')
\item[Script Synonym] A single-word identifier using only a-z letters, used in all Fountain scripts (e.g., ``TCHALLA'', ``PICARD'')
\end{description}

\begin{Important}
\textbf{When Synonyms Are Needed:} Script synonyms are only necessary when the display name doesn't work for both contexts. If the display name is already a single word that works in Fountain scripts, no synonym is required. For example, ``SPOCK'' could be both the display name and script identifier without needing a separate synonym.
\end{Important}

\subsection{Display Name Requirements}
\begin{itemize}
\item Maximum length: 16 characters
\item Must use only characters from the Minifont character set
\item Can include spaces, hyphens, apostrophes, and accented characters
\item Examples: ``King T'Challa'', ``Jean-Luc Picard'', ``Obi-Wan Kenobi''
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Script Synonym Requirements}
\begin{itemize}
\item Must be a single word (no spaces)
\item Must use only letters a-z (no length limit)
\item Cannot be any word reserved in the Fountain grammar (see Appendix~\ref{app:reserved-words})
\item Used in all Fountain script commands: \fountain{Enter TCHALLA at "Location"}, \fountain{TCHALLA speaks}, \fountain{TCHALLA walks}
\item Examples: ``TCHALLA'', ``PICARD'', ``KENOBI'', ``SPOCK'', ``KIRK''
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Database Configuration}
Each character must have both names properly configured in the character database:
\begin{description}
\item[First Column] Display name (16 characters max, Minifont set)
\item[Synonym Column] Script synonym (single word, a-z only)
\end{description}

\subsection{Script Usage}
In Fountain scripts, always use the script synonym:

\begin{SimpleExample}
\begin{FountainExample}
\stagedir{Enter TCHALLA at "Wakanda Palace".}
\speaker{TCHALLA}
\dialogue{Wakanda forever!}
\stagedir{TCHALLA walks to "Throne Room".}
\end{FountainExample}
\end{SimpleExample}

The display name ``King T'Challa'' will appear on screen, but the script uses ``TCHALLA'' for all commands.
\textbf{✓ Best Practice}
\textbf{Character Naming Best Practices:}
\begin{itemize}
\item Use descriptive display names that players will recognize
\item Keep script synonyms short and memorable
\item Use consistent naming patterns across your project
\item Test character names to ensure they work in all contexts
\end{itemize}
\begin{AntiPattern}
\textbf{Character Naming Mistakes:}
\begin{description}
\item[Reserved words] Using \fountain{PLAYER}, \fountain{ACTOR}, or other reserved terms as synonyms
\item[Special characters] Using numbers, hyphens, or spaces in script synonyms
\item[Inconsistent casing] Mixing upper and lower case in script synonyms
\item[Too long names] Display names exceeding 16 characters
\end{description}
\end{AntiPattern}

\section{Entering Characters}
\paragraphbreak

Every character\index{character}\index{character!entry} must be explicitly entered into the scene, even if they're expected to be present:

\begin{SimpleExample}
\begin{FountainExample}
\stagedir{Enter GEORGE at "George House". Enter MARY at "Town Square".}
\end{FountainExample}
\end{SimpleExample}

This ensures the game engine can properly position and manage characters.

\textbf{✓ Best Practice}
\textbf{Character Entry Best Practices:}
\begin{itemize}
\item Always enter characters at specific named locations
\item Enter characters before they speak or act
\item Use descriptive location names that exist on the map
\item Enter multiple characters in logical order (main characters first)
\end{itemize}


\begin{AntiPattern}
\textbf{Character Entry Mistakes:}
\begin{description}
\item[Missing entry] Characters speaking without being entered
\item[Invalid locations] Using location names that don't exist on the map
\item[Out of order] Characters speaking before being entered
\item[Inconsistent positioning] Characters appearing in impossible locations
\end{description}
\end{AntiPattern}

\section{Character Facing Directions}
\paragraphbreak

Characters can face one of four cardinal directions using the \fountain{faces} command:

\begin{SimpleExample}
\begin{FountainExample}
\stagedir{ACTOR faces north.}
\stagedir{ACTOR faces south.}
\stagedir{ACTOR faces east.}
\stagedir{ACTOR faces west.}
\end{FountainExample}
\end{SimpleExample}

\textbf{Valid Facing Directions:} Only the four cardinal directions are supported: \fountain{north}, \fountain{south}, \fountain{east}, \fountain{west}. Characters cannot face other actors, objects, or locations.

\textbf{✓ Best Practice}
\textbf{Facing Direction Best Practices:}
\begin{itemize}
\item Use cardinal directions only: north, south, east, west
\item Face characters toward the direction they need to look
\item Consider the map layout when choosing facing directions
\item Use facing to show character attention and focus
\end{itemize}


\begin{AntiPattern}
\textbf{Facing Direction Mistakes:}
\begin{description}
\item[Facing actors] \fountain{ACTOR faces PLAYER} (invalid)
\item[Facing objects] \fountain{ACTOR faces the door} (invalid)
\item[Facing locations] \fountain{ACTOR faces the throne} (invalid)
\item[Invalid directions] \fountain{ACTOR faces northeast} (invalid)
\end{description}
\end{AntiPattern}

\subsection{Common Pitfall: Facing Toward Objects}

A common mistake is trying to make characters face toward specific objects or people. Remember: characters can only face cardinal directions, not toward things.

\begin{AntiPattern}
\textbf{The "Facing Toward" Pitfall:}
\begin{description}
\item[Incorrect] \fountain{ACTOR faces toward the door} (trying to face an object)
\item[Incorrect] \fountain{ACTOR faces the player} (trying to face a person)
\item[Incorrect] \fountain{ACTOR looks at the throne} (trying to look at an object)
\item[Incorrect] \fountain{ACTOR turns toward the window} (trying to turn toward an object)
\end{description}
\end{AntiPattern}

\textbf{✓ Best Practice}
\textbf{Correct Approach:}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Determine direction}: Figure out which cardinal direction the object/person is in
\item \textbf{Face that direction}: Use \fountain{ACTOR faces north/south/east/west}
\item \textbf{Use movement}: Have the character walk to a position where they naturally face the target
\item \textbf{Consider positioning}: Place characters so they naturally face the right direction
\end{itemize}


\begin{SimpleExample}
\subsubsection{Example: Character needs to face a door on the north wall}
\textbf{Incorrect:}
\begin{FountainExample}
\stagedir{ACTOR faces toward the door.}
\end{FountainExample}
\end{SimpleExample}

\begin{SimpleExample}
\textbf{Correct:}
\begin{FountainExample}
\stagedir{ACTOR faces north.}
\end{FountainExample}
\end{SimpleExample}

\begin{SimpleExample}
\subsubsection{Example: Character needs to face another character who is east of them}
\textbf{Incorrect:}
\begin{FountainExample}
\stagedir{ACTOR faces PLAYER.}
\end{FountainExample}
\end{SimpleExample}

\begin{SimpleExample}
\textbf{Correct:}
\begin{FountainExample}
\stagedir{ACTOR faces east.}
\end{FountainExample}

\subsection{Movement Types}
\paragraphbreak

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Walk to location}: Direct path to a named point
\item \textbf{Walk by direction}: Move a specific number of tiles in a direction
\item \textbf{Compound movement}: Multiple movements in sequence using "X, then Y" syntax
\item \textbf{Start walking}: Begin walking animation (for simultaneous movement)
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Walk vs. Start Walking}

The Fountain language provides two movement commands for different purposes:

\begin{description}
\item[\fountain{ACTOR walks to "Location"}] Immediate movement to destination (shorthand for "starts walking" + "wait for")\footnote{For detailed movement syntax and examples, see \autoref{sec:character-movement}.}
\item[\fountain{ACTOR starts walking to "Location"}] Begin walking animation (allows other actions during movement)
\end{description}

\textbf{Understanding "walks to":}
The \fountain{walks to} command is essentially shorthand for the most common case: \fountain{starts walking} followed by \fountain{Wait for ACTOR}. It's designed for simple, direct movement where you want the character to complete their movement before any other actions occur.

\textbf{When to use "starts walking":}
\begin{itemize}
\item Multiple characters moving simultaneously
\item Actions that should happen during movement
\item Creating more natural, flowing scenes
\item When you want to separate movement from other actions
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Example - Multiple Characters Moving Simultaneously:}
\begin{FountainExample}
\stagedir{Enter PLAYER at "Door". PLAYER starts walking to "Center".}
\stagedir{Enter ALICE at "Door". ALICE starts walking to "Left Position".}
\stagedir{Enter BOB at "Door". BOB walks to "Right Position".}
\stagedir{All characters face north.}
\end{FountainExample}

\textbf{Example - Sequential Movement with Timing:}
\begin{FountainExample}
\stagedir{PLAYER starts walking to "Stage Left".}
\stagedir{ALICE starts walking to "Stage Center".}

\subsection{Action Duration Patterns}

Many character actions support three different duration patterns:

\begin{description}
\item[\fountain{ACTOR action}] Perform the action once (e.g., \fountain{ACTOR gestures})
\item[\fountain{ACTOR starts action}] Begin the action and continue until stopped (e.g., \fountain{ACTOR starts gesturing})
\item[\fountain{ACTOR stops action}] Stop the ongoing action and return to idle (e.g., \fountain{ACTOR stops gesturing})
\end{description}

\textbf{✓ Best Practice}
\textbf{Action Duration Best Practices:}
\begin{description}
\item[Use "starts" for ongoing actions] Actions that should continue until explicitly stopped
\item[Use "stops" to end ongoing actions] Always pair "starts" with corresponding "stops"
\item[Use simple form for one-time actions] Actions that happen once and complete immediately
\item[Plan action sequences] Consider the timing and flow of character actions in scenes
\item[Stop actions are idempotent] Calling \fountain{ACTOR stops action} when the action is already stopped has no effect
\end{description}

\textbf{Idempotent Behavior:}
Stop actions are idempotent - calling \fountain{ACTOR stops action} when the action is already stopped has no effect. This allows scripts to safely stop actions without checking their current state.

\textbf{Examples:}
\begin{FountainExample}
\stagedir{Player stops dancing}   ; Safe even if not dancing
\stagedir{Player stops waving}    ; Safe even if not waving
\stagedir{Player stops flying}    ; Safe even if not flying
\end{FountainExample}

\textbf{Relative Positioning:}

\begin{itemize}
\item \fountain{ACTOR is at 3 right of "Location"}: Position 3 tiles to the right of a named location
\item \fountain{ACTOR is at 2 left of "Location"}: Position 2 tiles to the left of a named location
\item \fountain{ACTOR is at 1 up of "Location"}: Position 1 tile above a named location
\item \fountain{ACTOR is at 4 down of "Location"}: Position 4 tiles below a named location
\end{itemize}

Characters can express emotions through visual emotes that appear above their heads:

\begin{FountainExample}
\stagedir{ACTOR looks sweating.}
\end{FountainExample}

\begin{FountainExample}
\stagedir{ACTOR looks confused.}
\end{FountainExample}

\begin{FountainExample}
\stagedir{ACTOR looks surprised.}
\end{FountainExample}

\begin{FountainExample}
\stagedir{ACTOR looks puzzled.}
\end{FountainExample}

\begin{FountainExample}
\stagedir{ACTOR looks alarmed.}
\end{FountainExample}

These emotes provide visual feedback for character emotions and can be used for both dramatic and comedic effect:
- \fountain{sweating}: Shows worry, anxiety, or working hard (sweat emote appears above character)
- \fountain{confused/puzzled}: Shows confusion, curiosity, or bewilderment (question mark ``?'' appears above character)
- \fountain{surprised/alarmed}: Shows surprise, shock, excitement, or dramatic revelation (exclamation mark ``!'' appears above character)

\textbf{Valid Emotion States:} Only these specific emotion states are supported by the parser: \fountain{sweating}, \fountain{confused}, \fountain{surprised}, \fountain{puzzled}, \fountain{alarmed}, \fountain{positive}, and \fountain{negative}.\footnote{For a complete reference of supported emotions and their usage, see \autoref{sec:character-emotions}.} Other descriptive terms like "wealthy," "wise," "determined," etc. are not valid and will cause parsing errors.

\chapter{The NPCStats File}
\label{chap:npcstats-file}

All character appearance, voice, and stats are defined in the \texttt{NPCStats.ods} file. This file provides detailed control over visual appearance, voice characteristics, and gameplay statistics for every character in the game.

\section{Column Descriptions}
\paragraphbreak
The character spreadsheet contains the following columns, each controlling specific aspects of character presentation:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Name} — The character's display name as it appears in dialogue and credits
\item \textbf{Comment} — Optional descriptive notes about the character's role or background
\item \textbf{Home} — The character's home location or affiliation
\item \textbf{Decal} — The character's base sprite type (human, sailor, Captain, Vizier, etc.)
\item \textbf{Body} — Clothing type: \texttt{tunic} (0) or \texttt{robe} (1) for humans; numeric (0-7) for sailors
\item \textbf{Head} — Head sprite variant (0-9 for humans, 0-14 for merfolk)
\item \textbf{Hair} — Hair color from the available palette
\item \textbf{Skin} — Skin tone from the available palette
\item \textbf{Clothing} — Clothing color from the available palette
\item \textbf{Speech Color} — Color of the character's dialogue balloons
\item \textbf{Voice Pitch} — Speech synthesis pitch (60-240, higher = higher pitch)
\item \textbf{Voice Speed} — Speech synthesis speed (80-120, higher = faster)
\item \textbf{Voice Bend} — Speech synthesis pitch variation (0-10)
\item \textbf{HP} — Hit points for combat encounters
\item \textbf{AC} — Armor class for combat encounters
\item \textbf{Gender} — Character gender (M/F/N for Male/Female/Non-binary)
\item \textbf{Nicks} — Alternative names or titles for the character
\item \textbf{Class} — Character class or role in the game
\item \textbf{Crowns} — Number of crowns the character carries
\item \textbf{Arrows} — Number of arrows the character carries
\item \textbf{Potions} — Number of potions the character carries
\item \textbf{Chalice} — Number of chalices the character carries
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Color Palette Usage}

The character spreadsheet uses a standardized color palette for consistent visual representation:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Peach}, \textbf{Orange}, and \textbf{Brown}: Generally used for skin colors
\item \textbf{Other colors}: Used for fantasy characters, aliens, or special cases
\end{itemize}

This system ensures consistent representation while allowing for the diverse range of characters found in modern media.

\subsection{Special Character Types}

Certain character types use specialized sprites that override the standard head and body settings:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Captain}: Uses a distinctive captain sprite with hat and uniform
\item \textbf{Princess}: Uses an elegant princess sprite with crown and formal attire
\item \textbf{Vizier}: Uses a wise advisor sprite with robes and staff
\item \textbf{Sailor}: Uses numbered body variants (0-7) for different sailor characters
\item \textbf{Merfolk}: Uses numbered head variants (0-14) for different merfolk faces
\item \textbf{Elder}: Uses a distinguished elder sprite with white hair and robes
\end{itemize}

These special types provide visual distinction for important characters while maintaining consistency within their roles.

\section{How the Game Uses the File}
The game engine reads the \texttt{NPCStats.ods} file to determine each character's appearance, voice, and statistics. This ensures consistent representation across all scenes and allows for easy updates and customization.

\chapter{Dialogue and Text}
\sectionbreak

\label{chap:dialogue}

Dialogue\index{dialogue}\index{dialogue!basics} is the primary way characters communicate in Fountain scripts. The system supports natural conversation flow, player-specific pronouns, emphasis, and off-camera dialogue.

\section{Basic Dialogue}
\paragraphbreak

Dialogue\index{dialogue}\index{dialogue!format} follows standard Fountain format. Note that individual dialogue blocks are limited to 192 characters (\gls{dialogue-length-limit}) for retro hardware compatibility:

\begin{FountainExample}
\slugline{INT SMALL TOWN - MAIN STREET}

\stagedir{Enter GEORGE at "Town Square". Enter MARY at "Visitor Position".}

\speaker{GEORGE}
\dialogue{Hi, I'm George. Welcome to our town!}

\speaker{MARY}
\dialogue{Thank you! It's lovely here.}
\end{FountainExample}

\section{Paragraph Breaks}

The pilcrow symbol (¶) creates paragraph breaks within dialogue blocks:

\begin{FountainExample}
\slugline{INT MYSTERY SHACK - LIVING ROOM}

\stagedir{Open on: STAN is at "Armchair". DIPPER is at "Couch Left". MABEL is at "Couch Right". SOOS is at "Workbench". Then...}

\speaker{STAN}
\dialogue{Listen carefully, kids. ¶ This Journal is important. ¶ We need to figure out what these symbols mean.}
\end{FountainExample}

This creates visual separation between sentences while keeping them in the same dialogue block. Paragraph breaks are useful for dramatic pauses and improved readability.

\section{Variable Basics}
\paragraphbreak

\gls{variable}s\index{variable}\index{variable!basics} store game state and can be referenced in expressions:

\begin{SimpleExample}
\begin{FountainExample}
\stagedir{\$quest-completed.}
\stagedir{\$player-karma.}
\stagedir{\$battle-count.}
\end{FountainExample}
\end{SimpleExample}

Variable names use hyphens and can be any length.

\textbf{✓ Best Practice}
\textbf{Variable Naming Best Practices:}
\begin{itemize}
\item Use descriptive names that clearly indicate the variable's purpose
\item Use hyphens to separate words: \fountain{\$quest-status}, \fountain{\$player-strength}
\item Use consistent naming patterns across your project
\item Initialize variables at the start of scenes when needed
\end{itemize}


\begin{AntiPattern}
\textbf{Variable Naming Mistakes:}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Unclear names}: \fountain{\$var1}, \fountain{\$temp}, \fountain{\$x}
\item \textbf{Inconsistent formatting}: \fountain{\$questStatus} (camelCase instead of kebab-case)
\item \textbf{Too long names}: \fountain{\$the-players-current-quest-status-value}
\item \textbf{Reserved words}: Using \fountain{\$player} or other reserved terms
\end{itemize}
\end{AntiPattern}

\paragraphbreak

\begin{Important}
\textbf{Variable Interpolation in Dialogue}: Currently, there is no general case for interpolating arbitrary \fountain{\$variable} values directly into dialogue text. Only predefined variables using the \fountain{~} prefix are supported for dialogue interpolation (e.g., \fountain{~player}, \fountain{~they}, \fountain{~sword}). If you need to display variable values in dialogue, consider using stage directions to set the context or use the predefined player variables. This feature could be added in the future if absolutely necessary.
\end{Important}

\section{Simple Syntax Example: Basic Variable Operations}

\begin{SimpleExample}
\begin{FountainExample}
\slugline{INT SHOP - COUNTER}

\stagedir{PLAYER is at "Shop Counter".}

\speaker{SHOPKEEPER}
\dialogue{That sword costs 50 gold pieces.}

\speaker{PLAYER}
\dialogue{I have 100 gold pieces.}

\stagedir{We hear "coins clink".}
\stagedir{\$gold becomes \$gold - 50.}

\speaker{PLAYER}
\dialogue{Now I have 50 gold pieces left.}
\end{FountainExample}
\end{SimpleExample}

\begin{Important}
There must be \textbf{zero or one} \fountain{Open on:} section per scene. All initial placements and states must be included in a single \fountain{Open on:} block. Multiple \fountain{Open on:} blocks in the same scene are not allowed and will cause errors.
\end{Important}

As you see, the player's \$gold variable was changed using the mathematical operation \fountain{\$gold - 50}, and the \fountain{We hear "coins clink"} command provides audio feedback.

\subsection{Label Reference (Using Double Quotes ")}

To \textbf{jump to} a label (reference an existing label), use double quotes ("):

\begin{SimpleExample}
\begin{FountainExample}
\slugline{INT TAVERN - MAIN ROOM}

\speaker{PLAYER}
\dialogue{What should I do?}
\parenthetical{to "Ask about cake"}
\dialogue{Yes, I'd love some cake!}
\parenthetical{to "Battle sequence"}
\dialogue{I'm ready to fight!}

\stagedir{\#Ask about cake}
\speaker{GEORGE}
\dialogue{Would you like some cake?}
\stagedir{GEORGE walks to "Kitchen".}

\stagedir{\#Battle sequence}
\speaker{MARY}
\dialogue{Prepare yourself!}
\stagedir{MARY draws sword.}
\end{FountainExample}
\end{SimpleExample}

\textbf{Label Reference Rules:}
\begin{itemize}
\item Use double quotes (") to reference existing labels
\item The label name must match exactly (including spaces)
\item References can be used in jump commands, transitions, and dialogue branching
\item Labels must be placed before they can be referenced
\item For player choices, use \fountain{(to "Label") Choice text} format
\item The PLAYER character typically presents choices to the player
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Linear Flow and Label Behavior:}
\begin{itemize}
\item Labels and their destinations appear \textbf{linearly} in the script after the decision point
\item Each choice leads to a specific label that contains the consequences of that choice
\item \textbf{Important}: Unless explicitly told otherwise, a scene will continue playing through labels as if they were not there
\item To prevent scenes from running into each other, labels typically:
  \begin{itemize}
  \item Jump to another scene: \fountain{> TO "New Scene Label"}
  \item Continue in another file: \fountain{Continued in "FileName" in "FolderName"}
  \item Return to the main game flow: \fountain{go to "Main Story"}
  \end{itemize}
\item This linear structure allows for clear branching while maintaining script readability
\end{itemize}

\textbf{✓ Best Practice}
\textbf{Label Reference Best Practices:}
\begin{itemize}
\item Always ensure the referenced label exists in the script
\item Use exact name matching (case-sensitive)
\item Test jump commands to verify they work correctly
\item Keep label names consistent between placement and reference
\end{itemize}


\begin{AntiPattern}
\textbf{Common Label Mistakes:}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Wrong syntax}: Using \fountain{\#Label} to jump (should be \fountain{"Label"})
\item \textbf{Wrong syntax}: Using \fountain{"Label"} to place (should be \fountain{\#Label})
\item \textbf{Missing label}: Referencing a label that doesn't exist
\item \textbf{Name mismatch}: Slight differences in label names
\item \textbf{Wrong choice format}: Using \fountain{go to "Label"} for player choices (should be \fountain{(to "Label") Choice text})
\end{itemize}
\end{AntiPattern}

\begin{itemize}

\item Keep choice text concise but descriptive
\end{itemize}

\subsection{False Choices for Enhanced Interactivity}

Sometimes you want to give players the illusion of choice even when both options lead to the same outcome. This creates a false sense of interactivity that makes dialogue feel more engaging:

\begin{FountainExample}
\slugline{INT TAVERN - MAIN ROOM}

\stagedir{Open on: INNKEEPER is at "Bar Counter". Then...}
\stagedir{Enter PLAYER at "Tavern Door".}

\speaker{INNKEEPER}
\dialogue{Welcome, traveler! What can I get you?}

\speaker{PLAYER}
\dialogue{What do you have?}
\parenthetical{to "Ask About Ale"}
\dialogue{Do you have any ale?}
\parenthetical{to "Ask About Wine"}
\dialogue{What wines do you serve?}

\stagedir{\#Ask About Ale}
\speaker{INNKEEPER}
\dialogue{We have the finest ale in the region!}

\stagedir{\#Ask About Wine}
\speaker{INNKEEPER}
\dialogue{We have the finest ale in the region!}

\speaker{PLAYER}
\dialogue{Perfect! I'll have some ale then.}
\end{FountainExample}

Both choices lead to the same response, but the player feels like they made a meaningful decision.

\section{Fountain Syntax Do's and Don'ts}
\label{sec:fountain-syntax-dos-and-donts}

\begin{Important}
\textbf{Critical Syntax Rules:}
\end{Important}

\textbf{Do's:}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Use "Then..." (three dots)}: \fountain{Open on: CHARACTER is at "Location". Then...} (correct)
\item \textbf{Quote location names}: \fountain{Enter PLAYER at "Main Door"} (correct)
\item \textbf{Quote pick-up items}: \fountain{PLAYER picks up "SWORD"} (correct)
\item \textbf{Use unquoted equipment}: \fountain{PLAYER equips SWORD} (correct)\footnote{See \autoref{app:equipment-items} for the complete list of valid equipment items.}
\item \textbf{Use supported emotions}: \fountain{CHARACTER looks sweating/surprised/confused/puzzled/alarmed} (correct)
\item \textbf{Use mononymous character names}: \fountain{Enter HERO at "Door"} (correct)
\item \textbf{Use proper scene headers}: \fountain{INT LOCATION - MAP NAME} (correct)
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Don'ts:}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Don't use "Then.." (two dots)}: \fountain{Open on: CHARACTER is at "Location". Then..} (incorrect)
\item \textbf{Don't forget location quotes}: \fountain{Enter PLAYER at Main Door} (incorrect)
\item \textbf{Don't quote equipment items}: \fountain{PLAYER equips "SWORD"} (incorrect)\footnote{Equipment items are predefined system items, not scene objects. See \autoref{sec:equipment-and-item-commands} for the syntax difference.}
\item \textbf{Don't forget pick-up quotes}: \fountain{PLAYER picks up SWORD} (incorrect - will fail)\footnote{Pick-up items must be quoted as they are scene objects. See \autoref{sec:equipment-and-item-commands} for details.}
\item \textbf{Don't use unsupported emotions}: \fountain{CHARACTER looks happy/sad/angry} (incorrect)
\item \textbf{Don't use character names with digits}: \fountain{Enter HERO1 at "Door"} (incorrect)
\item \textbf{Don't use incorrect scene format}: \fountain{INT LOCATION MAP NAME} (missing hyphen)
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Common Syntax Mistakes:}
\begin{AntiPattern}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Incorrect timing}: \fountain{Then..} instead of \fountain{Then...}
\item \textbf{Mixed quote usage}: Quoting equipment but not pick-up items
\item \textbf{Unsupported actions}: \fountain{CHARACTER shakes violently} (use \fountain{CHARACTER looks sweating})
\item \textbf{Incorrect movement}: \fountain{PLAYER walks 5 to the north} (use \fountain{PLAYER walks 5 north})
\item \textbf{Missing scene setup}: Characters appearing without explicit entry
\end{itemize}
\end{AntiPattern}

\chapter{Setting Entrance Navigation Points in Map Files}
\label{chap:entrance-navigation-points}

Entrance navigation points are special locations in a map that define where a character or the player can enter a scene. They are essential for connecting maps, handling exits, and providing precise stage directions in scripts.\footnote{For detailed character entry syntax, see \autoref{sec:character-entry}.}

\section{What is an Entrance Navigation Point?}
An Entrance navigation point is a named location in a map file, typically used as:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The arrival point when entering a scene from another map (e.g., through a door or portal)
  \item A reference mark for stage directions or scripted movement
\end{itemize}

\section{How to Set an Entrance Point}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Open your map in the map editor (such as Tiled).
  \item Add an object to the Object layer at the desired entrance location.
  \item In the object's properties, add a property named \texttt{Entrance} (case-sensitive).
  \item Set the value of \texttt{Entrance} to a unique, descriptive name in PascalCase (e.g., \texttt{WestDoor}, \texttt{MainGate}, \texttt{SecretPassage}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Best Practices}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Use PascalCase for entrance names (e.g., \texttt{NorthEntrance}, \texttt{CellarStairs}).
  \item Ensure each entrance name is unique within its map.
  \item Place the entrance object exactly where the player or character should appear.
  \item Document entrance points in your map notes or design documents for clarity.
\end{itemize}

\section{Example}
Suppose you want to create an entrance called \texttt{WestDoor} in the town hall map:
\begin{FountainExample}
\stagedir{Object Layer:}
\stagedir{  Object at (x=3, y=12)}
\stagedir{    Properties:}
\stagedir{      Entrance: WestDoor}
\end{FountainExample}

In Tiled, you would add an object at the desired tile and set its custom property \texttt{Entrance} to \texttt{WestDoor}.

\section{Referencing Entrance Points}
Entrance points can be referenced by:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Exits in other maps (e.g., an exit property might specify \texttt{Atsirav/TownHall/WestDoor})
  \item Stage directions in Fountain scripts (e.g., \texttt{Enter PLAYER at WestDoor})
\end{itemize}

This system allows for flexible, maintainable navigation and scripting across complex maps.

\chapter{Line-Level Syntax (Lexer Reference)}
\label{app:lexer-syntax}

This appendix lists every \emph{top-level line form} the Fountain compiler recognizes at the start of a line (after optional leading space), in the same sense as the lexer in \texttt{SkylineTool/src/fountain.lisp}. Each subsection states the rule, then gives a short colloquial example you can paste into a real script file (replace map and character names with assets that exist in your project).

\section{Blank Lines and Stage-Direction Blocks}

A non-empty line that is \textbf{not} a scene header, speaker line, transition, label, synopsis, metadata, or note begins a \emph{stage-direction} block. Following lines are appended to that block until a completely blank line flushes it to the stage-direction parser.

\begin{FountainExample}
\stagedir{Open on: PLAYER is at "WestDoor". Then...}
\stagedir{Enter NORVILLE at "Hallway". NORVILLE faces east.}
\stagedir{NORVILLE walks to "Clerk Desk". Beat.}

\speaker{NORVILLE}
\dialogue{Evening. I need whatever passes for common sense around here.}
\end{FountainExample}

\section{Scene Headers: Map Scenes and BLOB Screens}

\textbf{Map scene} (requires \texttt{Source/Maps/<Locale>/<Map>.tmx}):

\begin{FountainExample}
\slugline{INT ATSIRAV - ATSIRAV TOWN HALL}
\end{FountainExample}

\noindent The lexer also accepts \fountain{INT.} and \fountain{EXT.} (period immediately after the keyword) with the same slugline rules as \fountain{INT} and \fountain{EXT}.

\textbf{BLOB fullscreen} (slugline ends with \fountain{(BLOB)}; compiles to a symbol of the form \texttt{Blob\_}\textit{PascalCaseSlug}\texttt{\_ID} and the scripted blob dismiss path):

\begin{FountainExample}
\slugline{INT INTERWORLDLY (BLOB)}
\end{FountainExample}

\textbf{Optional scene number} in the slugline (after the map name, before a closing \texttt{\#}\ldots\texttt{\#}) is used when resolving script IDs; omit it in drafts if you do not need a fixed scene number yet.

\section{Speaker Lines and Dialogue}

An all-caps line (with optional parenthetical) starts dialogue. The next non-indented lines accumulate as the spoken line until a blank line or another lexer token.

\textbf{On-camera:}

\begin{FountainExample}
\speaker{PLAYER}
\dialogue{All right. Say it plain, and say it once.}
\end{FountainExample}

\textbf{Off-camera labels} (only these spellings are accepted in parentheses): \fountain{(VO)}, \fountain{(V/O)}, \fountain{(O/C)}, \fountain{(OC)}.

\begin{FountainExample}
\speaker{NARRATOR (VO)}
\dialogue{The hall smelled of sealing-wax, old ledgers, and stubborn pride.}

\speaker{GUARD (O/C)}
\dialogue{You can go in. Mind your tone.}
\end{FountainExample}

\section{Dialogue Branching with \fountain{(to ...)}}

After a speaker line, a line beginning with \fountain{(to } introduces a branch target. The option name becomes a \texttt{ScriptLabel\_\ldots} destination (except \fountain{CONTINUE}, which keeps the linear flow).

\begin{FountainExample}
\speaker{CLERK}
\dialogue{Do you want the long answer or the honest one?}

\stagedir{(to HonestAnswer)}
\speaker{PLAYER}
\dialogue{Honest. Always.}

\stagedir{(to continue)}
\speaker{CLERK}
\dialogue{Then we do this the hard way.}
\end{FountainExample}

\section{Right-Flush Lines: Transitions and Endings (\texttt{> ...})}

These lines start with \fountain{>} (greater-than). Recognized forms include:

\begin{itemize}
\item \fountain{> FADE OUT} --- fade to black (same effect as \fountain{> FADE TO BLACK} for the engine target \fountain{black})
\item \fountain{> FADE TO BLACK} / \fountain{> FADE TO WHITE} / \fountain{> FADE TO} \emph{color-word}
\item \fountain{> TO ...} --- go to label (label support is still flagged in generated Forth; see issue references in compiler output)
\item \fountain{> FINIS.}, \fountain{> THE END.}, \fountain{> END.} --- end the script file (equivalent endings)
\item \fountain{> END OF LINE.} --- reboot to title hardware path
\item \fountain{> RETURN TO TITLE PAGE.} / \fountain{> RETURN TO TITLE CARD.} --- return to title mode (the lexer matches the prefix \fountain{RETURN TO TITLE } including the trailing space, so another word must follow \fountain{TITLE}; see \texttt{Source/Scripts/Global/Credits.fountain})
\item \fountain{> GAME OVER - WON.} / \fountain{> GAME OVER - LOST.} / \fountain{> GAME OVER - TITLE.} --- end states (only \fountain{WON}, \fountain{LOST}, and \fountain{TITLE} are accepted after the dash)
\end{itemize}

\begin{FountainExample}
\stagedir{We hear "door-shut". Beat.}
\sceneendcmd{> FADE TO BLACK.}
\sceneendcmd{> FINIS.}
\end{FountainExample}

\section{Scene Labels, Synopsis Lines, and Notes}

\begin{itemize}
\item \fountain{\# LABELNAME} --- scene label token (compiler emits a placeholder jump; full label linking is tracked separately)
\item \fountain{= Synopsis text} --- treated as a structured note (emitted as a comment in Forth)
\item \fountain{[[ ... ]]} --- note on one line; unmatched \fountain{[[} continues across lines until \fountain{]]}
\end{itemize}

\begin{FountainExample}
\stagedir{[[ TODO: tighten clerk beat once VO is recorded ]]}

\stagedir{= Second act hinge: player chooses honesty vs. politics.}

\stagedir{\# TownHallConfrontation}
\end{FountainExample}

\section{Fountain Metadata Lines (Title Block)}

Lines of the form \fountain{Key: value} where \fountain{Key} is one of \fountain{title}, \fountain{credit}, \fountain{author}, \fountain{format}, \fountain{source}, \fountain{date}, \fountain{contact} become metadata. A continuation line that does not start a new key appends to the previous value until a blank line or a non-continuation line.

\begin{FountainExample}
title: Atsirav --- Clerk Scene (draft)
author: Your Name Here
contact: scriptnotes@@example.invalid
\end{FountainExample}

\subsection{Character Inventory Management}

\subsection{Item Command Do's and Don'ts}

\begin{Important}
\textbf{Item Command Syntax Rules:}
\end{Important}

\textbf{Do's:}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Quote pick-up items}: \fountain{PLAYER picks up "WEAPON"} (correct)
\item \textbf{Use predefined equipment names}: \fountain{PLAYER equips WEAPON} (correct)
\item \textbf{Use articles with pick-up}: \fountain{PLAYER picks up the "WEAPON"} (correct)
\item \textbf{Use articles with equip}: \fountain{PLAYER equips the WEAPON} (correct)
\item \textbf{Test item names}: Ensure they exist in your game's inventory system
\item \textbf{Use descriptive names}: Make item names clear and recognizable
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Don'ts:}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Don't quote equipment items}: \fountain{PLAYER equips "WEAPON"} (incorrect)\footnote{Equipment items are predefined system items, not scene objects. See \autoref{sec:equipment-and-item-commands} for the syntax difference.}
\item \textbf{Don't forget quotes for pick-up}: \fountain{PLAYER picks up WEAPON} (incorrect - will fail to parse)\footnote{Pick-up items must be quoted as they are scene objects. See \autoref{sec:equipment-and-item-commands} for details.}
\item \textbf{Don't use non-existent items}: Only use items defined in your inventory system
\item \textbf{Don't use special characters}: Avoid spaces, hyphens, or special symbols in item names
\item \textbf{Don't mix syntax}: Pick-up and equip commands have different syntax requirements
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Common Mistakes:}
\begin{AntiPattern}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Incorrect pick-up syntax}: \fountain{PLAYER picks up WEAPON} (missing quotes)
\item \textbf{Incorrect equip syntax}: \fountain{PLAYER equips "WEAPON"} (unnecessary quotes)
\item \textbf{Non-existent items}: \fountain{PLAYER picks up "MAGIC WAND"} (if not in inventory)

\section{Weather and Lighting Commands}

\begin{Important}
\textbf{Default Lighting}: All scenes are normally-lit by default unless otherwise specified in the \fountain{Open on:} clause. Lighting can be changed during the scene using \fountain{It becomes} commands.
\end{Important}

\begin{itemize}
\item \fountain{It is clear}: Clear weather
\item \fountain{It is raining}: Rainy weather
\item \fountain{It is clear around here}: Clear weather locally
\item \fountain{It is raining around here}: Rainy weather locally
\item \fountain{It is bright/dark/dim around here}: Lighting conditions locally
\item \fountain{It is dark/bright/dim}: Lighting conditions
\item \fountain{It is night}: Night lighting (dark)
\item \fountain{It is normally lit}: Normal lighting
\item \fountain{It is brightly lit}: Painfully bright lighting (use sparingly)
\item \fountain{It is dimly lit}: Dim lighting
\item \fountain{It is red-lit/cyan-lit}: Colored lighting
\item \fountain{It becomes dark/bright/dim}: Change lighting during scene
\item \fountain{It slowly/quickly becomes dark/bright/dim}: Timed lighting change
\item \fountain{It becomes dark/bright/dim around here}: Change lighting locally
\end{itemize}

\textbf{✓ Best Practice}
\textbf{When to Set Lighting in Open on:}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Non-standard lighting}: Use \fountain{It is dark/dim/brightly-lit} for scenes that aren't normally-lit
\item \textbf{Atmospheric scenes}: Cemeteries, dungeons, night scenes, interrogation rooms
\item \textbf{Special effects}: Colored lighting, painfully bright environments
\item \textbf{Leave default}: Don't specify \fountain{It is normally-lit} unless needed for clarity
\end{itemize}

\section{Do/Done Block Commands}
\begin{itemize}
\item \fountain{do: STATEMENTS done.}: Execute multiple commands as a block (primarily for conditionals/loops)
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Important Note:}
Do/Done blocks are \textbf{not conditionals themselves} - they are grouping constructs used to organize multiple commands within conditional statements and loops.

\textbf{When to use do: done. blocks:}
\begin{itemize}
\item Inside conditional statements (\fountain{When}, \fountain{Unless}, \fountain{If})
\item Inside loops or repeated actions (\fountain{Repeat N times})
\item When you need structured control flow with multiple commands
\end{itemize}

\textbf{When NOT to use do: done. blocks:}
\begin{itemize}
\item Simple sequences of commands at the top level
\item Basic character actions and movements
\item Audio commands and timing
\item Camera movements
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Example - Correct Usage:}
\begin{FountainExample}
\stagedir{When \$gold ≥ 50, do:}
\stagedir{PLAYER equips WEAPON.}
\stagedir{MERCHANT gives POTION to PLAYER.}
\stagedir{done.}
\end{FountainExample}

\textbf{Example - Incorrect Usage:}
\begin{FountainExample}
\stagedir{PLAYER walks to "Center". PLAYER faces north. PLAYER looks surprised.}
\end{FountainExample}

\textbf{Example - Correct Alternative:}
\begin{FountainExample}
\stagedir{PLAYER walks to "Center". PLAYER faces north. PLAYER looks surprised.}
\end{FountainExample}

\textbf{Example - Repeat Syntax:}
\begin{FountainExample}
\stagedir{Repeat 3 times: PLAYER walks 1 east. PLAYER faces north. PLAYER looks surprised.}
\end{FountainExample}

\begin{itemize}
\item \fountain{STATEMENTS}: Multiple statements separated by semicolons
\end{itemize}

\chapter{\Phantasia{} Equipment Item Names}
\label{app:equipment-items}

\section{Equipment Item Rules}

In \Phantasia{}, equipment items follow specific naming conventions and syntax rules:

\begin{Important}
\textbf{Equipment Item Syntax}: Equipment items use unquoted, predefined mononyms in the \fountain{equips} command. Unlike pick-up items, equipment items do not require quotes.\footnote{For detailed command syntax, see \autoref{sec:equipment-and-item-commands}. For the complete list of available equipment items, see \autoref{app:equipment-items}.}
\end{Important}

\subsection{Naming Conventions}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Short Syntax Names}: Equipment items use short, mononymous names in Fountain commands (e.g., \fountain{SWORD}, \fountain{SHIELD}, \fountain{KNIFE})
\item \textbf{UI Display Names}: The game interface shows longer, descriptive names (e.g., "Sword", "Small Shield", "Leather Armor")
\item \textbf{Predefined List}: Equipment items are defined in the fountain.lisp parser and cannot be arbitrarily created\footnote{See \autoref{app:equipment-items} for the complete list of 128 equipment items available in \Phantasia{}.}
\item \textbf{Case Insensitive}: Item names are case-insensitive in practice, but documentation uses UPPERCASE for clarity
\item \textbf{No Special Characters}: Item names contain only letters and spaces (for multi-word items)
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Short Names vs. UI Names}

Equipment items have two naming conventions:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Syntax Names}: Short, mononymous names used in Fountain commands (e.g., \fountain{SWORD}, \fountain{SHIELD}, \fountain{KNIFE})
\item \textbf{UI Names}: Longer, descriptive names displayed in the game interface (e.g., "Sword", "Small Shield", "Leather Armor")
\end{itemize}

\begin{Important}
\textbf{Important}: When writing Fountain scripts, always use the short syntax names (e.g., \fountain{ACTOR equips SWORD}), not the longer UI display names.
\end{Important}

This difference reflects the parser's implementation where equipment items are predefined system items, while pick-up items are scene objects that must be found by name.

\chapter{Animation Fallback System}

\section{Overview}

When a character performs an action for which no specific animation is available, the game engine automatically falls back to a similar animation. This ensures that characters always have a visual representation, even when custom animations haven't been created for every possible action.

\section{Fallback Animation Table}

The following table shows which animation is used as a fallback when a specific action animation is not available:

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{|l|l|}
\hline
\textbf{Action} & \textbf{Fallback Animation} \\
\hline
Idle & No fallback (base state) \\
Climbing & Walking \\
Hurt & Idle \\
Flying & Walking \\
Knocked Back & Idle \\
Swimming & Walking \\
Use Equipment & Walking \\
Wading & Walking \\
Walking & Idle \\
Wave Arms & Walking \\
Gesture & Wave Arms \\
Sleep & Idle \\
Non-Interactive & Idle \\
Dance & Wave Arms \\
Panic & Dance \\
Special Walk with Shield & Walking \\
Special Idle with Shield & Idle \\
Boating & Idle \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\caption{Animation Fallback Mappings}
\label{tab:animation-fallbacks}
\end{table}

\section{Implementation Details}

The fallback system is implemented in the game engine's character composition system. When a character action is requested:

\begin{enumerate}
\item The engine first attempts to use the specific animation for the requested action
\item If no animation is available for that action, it looks up the fallback animation from the ActionFallback table
\item The fallback animation is then used instead
\item If the fallback animation is also unavailable, the process repeats until a valid animation is found
\end{enumerate}

\section{Design Philosophy}

The fallback system is designed to provide graceful degradation:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Idle} serves as the ultimate fallback for most static actions
\item \textbf{Walking} is used as fallback for movement-related actions
\item \textbf{Wave Arms} serves as fallback for expressive actions like gestures and dancing
\item \textbf{Dance} is used as fallback for panic, providing a more dynamic alternative than idle
\end{itemize}

This ensures that characters remain visually engaging even when custom animations are missing, while maintaining logical consistency between related actions.
